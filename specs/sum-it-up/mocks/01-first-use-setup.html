Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is a biological process used by plants, algae, and some bacteria to convert light energy into chemical energy. That energy is stored in sugars built from carbon dioxide and water, and oxygen is released as a by-product.

Overview

Most photosynthesis happens in chloroplasts, where the pigment chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light. The light reactions split water molecules, release oxygen, and produce ATP and NADPH. The Calvin cycle then uses that stored energy to fix carbon dioxide into sugars.

Both stages are tightly linked. When light is limited, the light reactions slow down and the Calvin cycle runs short of ATP and NADPH, which lowers the rate of sugar production across the whole leaf.

Importance

Photosynthesis supplies most of the energy used by life on Earth and maintains the oxygen content of the atmosphere. Crop yields depend directly on how efficiently plants carry out this process, which is why it is a major focus of agricultural research.

Factors affecting the rate

Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, and temperature all limit the rate. Each factor sets an upper bound, and the factor in shortest supply controls the overall speed.
