What it is

  • Converts light into chemical energy stored in sugars.
  • Used by plants, algae, and some bacteria. Releases oxygen.

How it works

  • Chlorophyll in chloroplasts absorbs red and blue light.
  • Light reactions split water and produce ATP and NADPH.
  • The Calvin cycle uses both to turn carbon dioxide into sugars.

Why it matters

  • Supplies most energy used by life on Earth.
  • Maintains atmospheric oxygen.
  • Drives crop yields, so it is a focus of farming research.

What limits it

  • Light, carbon dioxide, and temperature.
  • The factor in shortest supply sets the speed.

Overview

Photosynthesis converts light into chemical energy stored in sugars, releasing oxygen.

Light reactions

Chlorophyll absorbs light, water splits, and the cell makes ATP and NADPH. ATP is the cell's energy carrier. NADPH carries high-energy electrons.

Calvin cycle

Uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugars. Carbon fixation means turning a gas into solid organic matter.

How the stages link

Low light slows the light reactions, which starves the Calvin cycle and lowers sugar output across the leaf.

Importance

Supplies most energy for life, keeps atmospheric oxygen stable, and sets crop yields.

Limiting factors

Light, carbon dioxide, and temperature each set an upper bound. Limiting factor means the input in shortest supply controls overall speed.

Plants make their own food, kind of like tiny chefs.

They need three things: sunlight, water, and a gas from the air called carbon dioxide.

The green stuff in leaves catches the sunlight. It works like a solar panel. The plant uses that energy to cook the water and the gas into sugar, which is its food.

While it cooks, the plant lets out oxygen. That is the air we breathe, so plants help keep us alive too.

With less sunlight, the chef slows down and the plant makes less food.
